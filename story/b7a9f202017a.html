Government withdraws upfront IGST exemption on precious metal imports

Nominated agencies face immediate cash outflows on consignments of gold, silver, and platinum.

  • Upfront IGST exemptions on imported gold, silver, and platinum have been withdrawn.
  • Importers face higher upfront cash requirements, impacting liquidity across precious metal supply chains.
  • Whether jewellers pass on increased financing costs to retail buyers in domestic markets.
Gems & JewelleryTrade & Tariffs

What happened

The government has withdrawn the upfront Integrated Goods and Services Tax (IGST) exemption previously available to banks and nominated agencies importing precious metals. The rule change affects imports of gold, silver, and platinum. Under existing rules cited in the report, these imports have also been subject to a 3% customs levy since 1 April 2026.

Who is affected

banks and nominated bullion importing agencies
They must now pay taxes at the border rather than receiving an upfront waiver, increasing initial cash requirements.
jewellery manufacturers and retailers
Procurement of raw gold and silver becomes more cash-intensive, potentially raising inventory financing costs.
retail gold and silver buyers
Consumers may absorb modest price increases if retailers pass along higher financing costs.

Which sectors

  • Gems & Jewellery headwind — Withdrawing upfront exemptions increases working capital requirements and inventory holding costs across the supply chain.

The transmission mechanism

Plays out over weeks

  1. Government removes upfront IGST exemption on precious metal imports
  2. Importers must pay cash upfront at customs clearance
  3. Working capital needs rise, requiring additional short-term credit
  4. Carrying costs are absorbed by retailers or passed into retail premiums

When agencies import gold, silver, or platinum, customs authorities will now demand tax payment immediately upon entry. Over several days, importing banks and agencies must deploy liquid cash or bank lines to clear the consignments. Over several weeks, this capital blockage raises financing costs for downstream jewellers purchasing bullion. Over a few months, businesses either absorb the higher borrowing expenses in their gross margins or pass them to consumers through retail markups.

The bull case

What optimists are arguing

Bulls argue that legitimate importers will seamlessly reclaim the upfront payment through input tax credits, preventing any lasting operational harm while curbing tax avoidance.

Depends on:

  • Swift processing of input tax credits by tax authorities
  • Sustained domestic retail demand that easily absorbs minor carrying fees

The bear case

What pessimists are arguing

The bear case rests on concerns that locking up large sums of upfront cash will inflate borrowing costs for mid-tier jewellers and inadvertently encourage parallel grey-market imports.

Depends on:

  • Prolonged delays in cash recovery through input tax credits
  • Higher interest rates elevating the cost of short-term working capital
