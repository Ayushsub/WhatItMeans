What happened

On 25 September 2026, the RBI’s bulletin highlighted that India’s financial and external sectors remained resilient despite global risks. It noted a sharp rise in crude oil prices driven by West Asia conflicts and cited forex reserves of $765.9 billion, strong Q1 FY27 growth and a five‑year high in net FDI.

The transmission mechanism

Plays out over months

  1. step 1: West Asia conflict pushes crude oil prices up
  2. step 2: Higher oil prices raise import bills and cost of living
  3. step 3: Elevated import costs feed into CPI inflation
  4. step 4: RBI may keep policy rate steady, affecting banks' net interest margins
  5. step 5: Companies with energy exposure see higher expenses, reflected in earnings

The conflict in West Asia lifts global oil prices, which immediately raises the cost of imported fuel and energy. Within a few weeks, the higher import bill shows up in the consumer price index, putting upward pressure on inflation. If inflation stays near the RBI’s target, the central bank is likely to hold its policy rate, keeping banking margins stable; if inflation accelerates, a rate hike could compress margins. The cost increase then appears in corporate earnings as higher operating expenses, especially for energy‑intensive firms. The lag from oil price shock to earnings impact typically spans a few months.
