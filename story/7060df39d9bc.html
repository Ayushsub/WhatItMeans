Global commodity markets face shifts as de-dollarisation trends emerge

Global trade in crude oil and gold faces a shift away from US dollar dominance.

  • Global trade explores alternatives to the US dollar for pricing major commodities.
  • Indian importers could bypass dollar conversion costs when buying crude oil and metals.
  • The adoption rate of local currency settlement systems in bilateral trade agreements.
Metals & MiningOil & GasCrude & Energy

What happened

On September 12, 2026, reports highlighted the ongoing global discussions around de-dollarisation in international trade. Historically, key commodities like crude oil, natural gas, gold, and agricultural products have been priced almost exclusively in US dollars. Emerging trade agreements are now increasingly exploring non-dollar settlement mechanisms to reduce currency risks.

Who is affected

Indian energy importers
They could see reduced transaction costs if they can settle crude purchases directly in Indian Rupees or partner currencies instead of converting to US dollars first.
Commodity exporters
They face increased currency management complexity as they receive payments in a wider basket of local currencies instead of a single dominant currency.

Which sectors

  • Metals & Mining mixed — While local currency pricing reduces dollar-exchange risks, it introduces new hedging complexities across multiple regional currencies.
  • Oil & Gas tailwind — Direct bilateral currency payments for crude imports can lower transaction friction and protect against sudden dollar spikes.

The transmission mechanism

Plays out over quarters

  1. Bilateral trade agreements shift commodity pricing from US dollars to local currencies
  2. Importers bypass the need to purchase US dollars on the open market
  3. Transaction costs and double-conversion currency losses decrease for importing firms
  4. Corporate profit margins improve and domestic retail prices face less dollar-driven inflation pressure

When trade moves away from the US dollar, Indian importers do not need to convert Rupees to Dollars to buy commodities like crude oil. This direct settlement route eliminates intermediary conversion fees and shields companies from sudden spikes in the USD-INR exchange rate. The transmission lag is typically quarters, as long-term supply contracts must be renegotiated and clearing systems established.

The bull case

What optimists are arguing

Bulls argue that settling trade in local currencies like the Rupee will lower transaction costs, reduce foreign exchange reserve drain, and stabilize domestic commodity prices against US dollar volatility.

Depends on:

  • Widespread acceptance of non-dollar currencies by major energy exporters
  • Establishment of robust bilateral clearing mechanisms

The bear case

What pessimists are arguing

The bear case rests on the view that abandoning the highly liquid US dollar will increase transaction friction, raise hedging costs for less liquid currencies, and complicate global supply chain accounting.

Depends on:

  • High volatility in alternative local currencies
  • Lack of deep financial markets for non-dollar hedging instruments

Jargon, in plain words

De-dollarisation
The process where countries reduce their reliance on the US dollar as a dominant currency for international trade, bilateral payments, and foreign exchange reserves.
Bilateral trade
Trade conducted directly between two nations under mutually agreed terms, often using their respective local currencies.
