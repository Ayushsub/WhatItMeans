Debt funds offload ₹51 billion in government bonds following RBI rate hike

A ₹378 billion quarterly exit is deepening across domestic debt portfolios.

  • Debt funds sold ₹51 billion of sovereign bonds after RBI rate hike.
  • Higher supply lowers bond prices and pushes market borrowing yields higher.
  • Watch whether institutional selling extends toward projected $4.5 billion levels.
Asset managementBanking

What happened

Indian asset managers sold a net ₹51 billion ($527.08 million) of government bonds across three sessions following an interest rate increase by the Reserve Bank of India. This extension comes on top of ₹378 billion in net sales recorded during the July-September quarter. The persistent selling points to a broader divestment trend projected to reach $4.5 billion.

Who is affected

Debt mutual fund investors
Face potential short-term declines in net asset values as bond prices fall.
Sovereign debt issuers
Confront upward pressure on borrowing costs as secondary market yields rise.

Which sectors

  • Asset management headwind — Bond price declines and debt fund redemptions reduce total assets under management and fee income.
  • Banking mixed — Rising bond yields trigger treasury mark-to-market losses on investment books, though higher lending rates may support margins.

The transmission mechanism

Plays out over weeks

  1. RBI raises benchmark interest rates
  2. Existing fixed-coupon government bonds become less attractive relative to new yields
  3. Mutual funds offload sovereign bonds to curb duration risk or meet redemptions
  4. Surplus paper supply depresses bond prices and pushes market yields higher

When the central bank hikes policy rates, newly issued bonds offer higher returns, causing older, lower-coupon bonds to fall in value. Within days of the policy announcement, asset managers begin selling sovereign securities to cut portfolio duration and limit paper losses. This surge in supply hits the debt market over several weeks, driving secondary yields higher and lowering bond prices across the curve. Over a period of quarters, these yield adjustments raise general funding costs for borrowers and reflect as lower returns in debt fund account statements.

Market reaction

Asset managers sold a net ₹51 billion ($527.08 million) of government bonds in the three sessions following the policy decision, adding to sales of ₹378 billion in the July-September period.

The bull case

What optimists are arguing

Bulls argue that the current selloff will reset yields at attractive levels, drawing in long-term investors such as insurers and pension funds once rate stability returns.

Depends on:

  • The RBI nearing the end of its rate-tightening cycle
  • Institutional demand reviving as benchmark yields peak

The bear case

What pessimists are arguing

The bear case rests on concerns that sustained policy tightening will prompt retail redemptions from debt funds, forcing mutual funds to dump more paper into an illiquid market.

Depends on:

  • Subsequent policy rate increases by the central bank
  • Persistent redemption pressures on short- and medium-duration funds
