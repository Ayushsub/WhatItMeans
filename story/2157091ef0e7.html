RBI shortens export repatriation window to nine months to boost dollar liquidity

Indian exporters must now bring back foreign revenues six months faster than before.

  • RBI cut the export earnings repatriation deadline from 15 to nine months.
  • It forces quicker dollar inflows, boosting local foreign exchange liquidity and easing bank approvals.
  • What to watch is how exporters manage cash flows and rupee-dollar exchange rates.
Export-oriented sectors (like IT and Textiles)Banking & FinancialsCredit & Liquidity

What happened

On September 25, 2026, the Reserve Bank of India shortened the deadline for exporters to bring back their foreign earnings from 15 months to nine months. To ease this transition, the central bank also delegated more approval powers to authorised dealer banks. This policy reversal aims to accelerate foreign exchange inflows and reduce regulatory bottlenecks.

Who is affected

Indian exporters
They face tighter working capital cycles as they must repatriate foreign sales proceeds six months earlier than previously allowed.
Authorised dealer banks
They gain greater administrative powers to approve transactions, reducing processing delays but increasing compliance responsibilities.
Currency market participants
They may see improved dollar liquidity in the domestic market, potentially easing short-term pressure on the rupee.

Which sectors

  • Export-oriented sectors (like IT and Textiles) headwind — Companies have less flexibility to hold foreign currency abroad, potentially impacting their cash management and hedging strategies.
  • Banking & Financials tailwind — Commercial banks receive more operational authority over trade transactions and will handle faster foreign exchange inflows.

The transmission mechanism

Plays out over months

  1. RBI reduces the export repatriation deadline from 15 to nine months
  2. Exporters are forced to convert foreign currency earnings into rupees sooner
  3. Dollar inflows into the domestic banking system accelerate
  4. Domestic dollar liquidity increases, easing pressure on the rupee's exchange rate

The trigger is the RBI's policy change, which immediately shortens the legal window for holding export proceeds abroad. Within weeks to months, as outstanding invoices mature, exporters must repatriate their dollars faster, directly increasing the supply of foreign currency in the domestic banking system. This influx of dollars helps commercial banks manage foreign exchange liquidity more efficiently. Over the next few quarters, this steady stream of inflows can help stabilise the rupee against major currencies, though exporters may feel a squeeze on their credit cycles.

The bull case

What optimists are arguing

Optimists argue that the move will significantly bolster India's foreign exchange reserves and stabilise the rupee by ensuring a steady, predictable flow of dollars back into the country.

Depends on:

  • Global demand for Indian exports remaining stable
  • Banks efficiently processing the newly delegated approvals without administrative delays

The bear case

What pessimists are arguing

Bears argue that the shortened window will strain the working capital of small and medium exporters, who often rely on longer credit terms to compete globally.

Depends on:

  • Exporters facing payment delays from international buyers
  • Increased compliance costs for businesses adapting to the tighter timeline
