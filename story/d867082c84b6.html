India's forex reserves hit $740.8bn, boosting rupee and bank liquidity

RBI's $136.4 billion dollar inflow strains its liquidity tools

  • Record reserves lift rupee
  • Banks gain extra lending capacity
  • Watch RBI's liquidity management
banking & financialsCurrencyCredit & Liquidity

What happened

By the end of August India’s foreign‑exchange reserves rose to a record $740.8 billion, according to the Economic Times. The same day Mint reported that the RBI’s dollar mobilisation drive attracted $136.4 billion of foreign deposits, creating excess liquidity in the banking system. Both developments have reinforced the rupee’s value against the dollar.

The transmission mechanism

Plays out over weeks

  1. step 1: record reserves and $136.4bn inflow swell RBI’s foreign‑currency holdings
  2. step 2: larger reserves boost rupee confidence and raise banks’ liquidity coverage ratio (LCR)
  3. step 3: higher LCR frees up funding for new loans or lower rates
  4. step 4: increased loan supply can lift bank earnings and support economic activity

The surge in reserves first strengthens the rupee, which reduces import‑cost pressure. At the same time, banks see their LCR rise because more foreign currency can be counted as high‑quality liquid assets. With a healthier LCR, banks are less constrained by regulatory limits and can extend more credit or trim margins. The effect on earnings appears after a lag of a few weeks as new loans are disbursed and interest accrues.

Jargon, in plain words

foreign‑exchange reserves
the stock of foreign currencies and gold held by the central bank
liquidity coverage ratio (LCR)
a regulatory metric that requires banks to hold enough high‑quality liquid assets to survive a 30‑day cash outflow stress
open market operations (OMO)
central‑bank actions of buying or selling securities to inject or withdraw liquidity from the banking system
